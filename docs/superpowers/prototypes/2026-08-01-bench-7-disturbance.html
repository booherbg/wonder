Disturbance

Wonder has no fire, no flood, no storm, no blight. Nothing is ever cleared, so succession runs one way and stops: islands converge and settle. This bench adds disturbance to a patch-occupancy model and asks two questions. Does diversity actually peak at intermediate disturbance — the textbook claim, which is genuinely contested? And does whatever effect exists survive being softened into something Wonder could ship, where nothing dies as a visible event and everything expresses as a rate?

The system

A patch-occupancy model — Tilman's competition–colonisation trade-off made cell by cell so it can be drawn. A 64 × 64 torus, one individual per cell. Species are ranked: a better competitor displaces a worse one it reaches, always, with no contest. The only thing a worse competitor can be given in exchange is a higher colonisation rate, and the strength of that exchange is a dial. Disturbance clears or suppresses discs of cells at random, and the question is what that does to diversity.

Parts

cell
One site on the torus, holding at most one individual. Occupancy is the whole state; there is no biomass, no age, no size.
competitive rank
A strict ordering. Species 0 beats everyone, species 1 beats everyone but 0, and so on. Contests are decided by rank alone.
colonisation rate c
How fast a species throws propagules into neighbouring cells. Spans 0.02 to 0.55 across the species set.
trade-off strength
The correlation between rank and colonisation rate, from 0 to 1. The set of rate values is fixed; only which species gets which value changes. That is what makes trade-off = 0 a clean control rather than "and also slower".
disturbance
At rate D per tick, a disc of radius R is chosen at random. lethal mode removes every occupant in it. peaceful mode leaves them standing but marks the cells suppressed for a recovery window, during which a suppressed occupant can be displaced by anyone, not just a better competitor.
seed bank
Two slots per cell. A displaced or disturbed occupant deposits its identity there; the entry decays with a half-life and germinates with a fixed probability against that decayed viability. Germination is read before the fresh propagule rain lands, or the bank becomes a mirror of the present instead of a memory of the past.
Hill diversity
The diversity number reported throughout, rather than raw species richness — it weights by abundance, so a species clinging to four cells does not count the same as one holding a quarter of the island.

Procedure — one tick

  1. Colonisation. Every occupied cell throws propagules at its neighbourhood at its species' rate. An arrival takes the cell if it is empty, if it is strictly better than the occupant, or if the occupant is currently suppressed.
  2. Background mortality. Each occupant dies with probability m, independent of everything else. This is the baseline turnover disturbance has to beat.
  3. Disturbance. With probability D, fire an event: pick a centre, clear or suppress the disc, then germinate from the bank and deposit the displaced.
  4. Recovery. Suppression counters tick down; bank entries age.
  5. Measure. Richness, Hill diversity, occupancy, and the count of regionally extinct species.

The claim under test, and why it is not safe

The Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis says diversity is maximised at intermediate frequency or intensity of disturbance. It is one of the most-cited ideas in community ecology and it is also one of the most seriously challenged. Fox (2013) argued in Trends in Ecology & Evolution that the IDH "has been refuted on both empirical and theoretical grounds, and so should be abandoned" — that empirical studies only rarely find the humped curve, and that all three mechanisms usually invoked to produce it (the competition–colonisation trade-off, the successional niche, and the storage effect) do not logically predict what they are believed to predict. The empirical side is starker still: in Mackey & Currie's review of the literature, the humped pattern appeared in only about 16% of 116 richness studies, 19% of 53 diversity studies and 11% of 28 evenness studies; non-significant relationships were the single most common outcome. Sheil & Burslem replied defending a broader reading of Connell's original idea, and the argument is not settled.

So this bench is not built to confirm a hump. It is built to find out whether this specific model, at these parameters, produces one — and to say so plainly if it does not. A flat or monotonic curve here is a real result, and it is the result that would save us from shipping a mechanic that does nothing.

The coexistence mechanism is the well-established one: a competition–colonisation trade-off. Levins & Culver (1971) showed two species can coexist regionally when the poorer competitor colonises faster; Tilman (1994) extended it to an unlimited number of species competing for a single limiting resource in a spatially subdivided habitat, provided inferior competitors colonise sufficiently better. That much is textbook and secure. What is contested is how often the trade-off is strong enough in nature — a recent review found conclusive support in roughly 15 of 34 studies — and Calcagno et al. (2006) had to relax the strict competitive hierarchy the classic models assume before the theory looked realistic. This bench uses the strict hierarchy, which is the most favourable case. If a hump does not appear here, it is unlikely to appear in a softer model.

Findings

Written after running it, not before — and two of these came out against what I expected. Default world unless stated: seed 7314, 6 species, trade-off 1.00, R = 4, mortality 0.006, recovery 25, half-life 60, germination 0.35; each sweep point is 5 replicate seeds, 400 ticks of burn-in and 200 ticks of averaging.

A hump appears, in all four series, at D ≈ 0.011

Effective species rises from 2.08 ± 0.38 at D = 0 to 4.52 ± 0.26 at D = 0.0114 for lethal disturbance without a seed bank, then falls to 1.99 at D = 0.15. The bench's own test — interior peak, clearing both endpoints by 1.96 pooled standard errors — returns hump for all four series, and all four peak at the same point. That is a 2.2× gain in effective diversity over the undisturbed island, at roughly 1% of the grid disturbed per tick. In this model, at these parameters, the intermediate-disturbance pattern is real and it is large.

The honest caveat is that the shape is parameter-dependent in exactly the way Fox's critique predicts. Raise background mortality from 0.006 to 0.02 and the D = 0 end already sustains 3.73 effective species on the trade-off alone; the peak only reaches 4.11, a 10% gain instead of 120%, and everything above D ≈ 0.03 is worse than doing nothing. The hump here is not evidence that intermediate disturbance is magic. It is evidence that an island with too little turnover is impoverished, and disturbance is one way to supply turnover. Wonder's island has no turnover at all, which is why this is still the right test — but it means the mechanic competes with simply giving plants a lifespan, and this bench cannot say which is better.

Peaceful mode survives — and beats lethal, which I did not expect

This was the decision-relevant question and the answer is better than "yes". Peaceful disturbance never empties a cell: the occupant stays alive and visible, stops reproducing for a recovery window, and becomes displaceable by anything that arrives. Its peak is 4.91 ± 0.11 against lethal's 4.52 ± 0.26 — outside the error bars, at the same disturbance rate, with a seed bank changing neither. I expected peaceful to be a weakened lethal. It is not.

The reason is visible in the occupancy strip: peaceful runs sit at ~96% occupancy against lethal's ~82%. Clearing a disc hands the ground to whoever arrives first, which is structurally biased toward the fastest coloniser — lethal disturbance is partly a coloniser subsidy. Suppression instead makes the incumbent beatable without surrendering the space, so the cell goes to whoever happens to reach it, competitor and coloniser alike. That is a milder, less directional perturbation, and here it maintains diversity better.

The recovery window did not do what I predicted either. Going from 25 to 60 ticks left the peak unchanged (4.84 vs 4.91, inside the error bars) but raised the low-D end sharply — 4.69 vs 4.18 at D = 0.0038. So the window does not control how much diversity peaceful disturbance can maintain; it controls how little disturbance you need to get there. For a game that is the more useful dial: a long recovery window buys the same ecology from rarer, gentler weather.

The seed bank is insurance, and peaceful mode mostly makes it redundant

At the peak the bank is worth nothing measurable: 4.52 → 4.59 lethal, 4.91 → 4.92 peaceful, well inside the error bars. What it changes is the high-disturbance tail, where regional extinctions accumulate. At D = 0.15, lethal diversity is 1.99 without the bank and 2.99 with it; without it, 4.0 of the 6 species are regionally extinct by the end of the run. A species at zero cells has no source anywhere on the island, so with the bank off richness is a ratchet that only turns down.

Over a long run — 4000 ticks, 8 species, D = 0.05, lethal — the bank posted 6 revivals and ended with 6 of 8 species and 5.48 effective species; the same run with the bank off posted 0 revivals and ended with 3 of 8 species and 2.78 effective species. That is the argument for the mechanic, and it is an argument about the tail, not the mean.

Two results here corrected me. First, half-life barely mattered: across 10, 20, 60, 120 and 300 ticks the revival counts were 4, 3, 6, 4 and 8 and richness held at 5–6 of 8 throughout. I expected a short half-life to kill the mechanic; it does not, because the cells that matter are re-disturbed soon after they are deposited. On/off is the decision, not the tuning. Second, and more useful: in peaceful mode the same 4000-tick run posted 0 revivals and only 2 extinction events, against 8 in lethal, and still ended at 5.62 effective species. Peaceful disturbance does not drive species to zero in the first place, so there is nothing for the bank to rescue. The seed bank is insurance against a risk that peaceful mode largely removes. It is worth shipping for the moment it creates — something returns that had been gone — not for the diversity number.

One structural note: germination only fires when a cell is disturbed, so at D = 0 the bank is completely inert and all four series report the identical 2.08. The seed bank is not an independent mechanic; it is a disturbance amplifier. If Wonder wants the bank to do anything without weather, it needs a second trigger — the tilling action.

The no-trade-off control: coexistence collapses, as expected

Confirmed, and hard. Setting trade-off strength to 0 shuffles the same set of colonisation rates across competitive ranks — the distribution of rates is unchanged, only the correlation with rank is destroyed. Diversity falls to 1.07 effective species at D = 0 and never exceeds 2.4 anywhere in the range; lethal disturbance without a bank sits at exactly 1.00 — a flat monoculture — across D = 0.035 to 0.085. Against 4.9 with the trade-off on, that is a collapse. Disturbance is not a diversity generator. It only reveals a trade-off that is already there.

And here is the most intellectually uncomfortable thing this bench produced, which is also the reason it was built this way. Three of the four no-trade-off series still pass the hump test. Peaceful with a seed bank "peaks" at 2.42 against 1.07 at D = 0 — interior, and comfortably significant, because the error bars at 5 replicates are tiny. It is a statistically real hump describing a community of two species that is functionally dead. If this page reported only "hump: yes", it would be reporting a fact and telling a lie. That gap between significant and meaningful is a large part of what Fox is complaining about, and it is why the verdict line under the chart prints the actual magnitudes next to the word.

What this does and does not tell us about Wonder

  • It tells us the mechanic has a mechanism. There is a defensible causal chain from "a patch got cleared" to "the island stayed diverse", and it is one Wonder can implement without any species-specific tuning: give plants a colonisation rate that is negatively correlated with their competitive rank, and disturbance does the rest.
  • It tells us the peaceful framing costs nothing, which was the real risk and the reason this bench exists. We do not have to choose between the pillar and the ecology, and on this evidence we would not choose the lethal version even if the pillar allowed it.
  • It tells us where to put the dial. D ≈ 0.01, R = 4, recovery 40–60. Above D ≈ 0.05 the model is losing species faster than it is maintaining them, in every configuration tested.
  • It does not tell us the effect size in Wonder. This model has one limiting resource, one axis of competition, a strict displacement hierarchy, no life stages, no age, no seasonality, no player, and no terrain. Wonder has all of those and they will all interact with this.
  • It does not settle the IDH, and it is not evidence against Fox. Fox's argument is precisely that a model can be built to produce a hump and that this does not make the hypothesis a general law — and the no-trade-off control above shows this bench producing statistically significant humps out of a dead community, which is his point made by accident. The claim here is only "this mechanism works in this model", which happens to be the only claim a game needs.
  • The regional-extinction counter is a warning, not a feature. At D = 0.15 with lethal disturbance and no bank, 4 of 6 species are gone by the end of a 600-tick run. An island left on high disturbance gets simpler, permanently. Any shipped version needs a bounded D, or an off-island source of propagules, or both.
  • It does not rule out the cheaper alternative. Most of the benefit here comes from turnover, not from turnover being spatially clustered. Before building weather, someone should run the same test with plant lifespans and no disturbance at all — this bench has the mortality slider to do it, and at m = 0.02, D = 0 it already reaches 3.73 of the 4.91 peak.

What the player would actually see

Nothing dies on screen. A weather front — or a tide, or a bloom of something, whatever the fiction ends up being — passes over a stretch of the island. The plants under it stop growing: no new shoots, no spread, a visible pause. Their colour dulls for a few dozen ticks. Nothing vanishes. Then the pause ends, and over the following minutes some of those cells hold something else, because a neighbour reached them while the incumbent was stalled.

The succession trace is blunt about what that actually looks like, and it is worth being clear-eyed. In one traced patch (lethal, D = 0.04, R = 4) the composition turned over by about 30% over 300 ticks — the dominant fell from 45% of the patch to 24%, a minor species climbed from 8% to 20%, another fell from 20% to 2% — but no species recolonised from zero. Everything that came back was already somewhere in the patch. The player's experience is therefore not "a new plant appeared"; it is "this hillside used to be mostly the tall red one and now it is mixed". That is a slower, quieter beat than the pitch implies, and it is the honest version. The dramatic case — a species that had genuinely vanished germinating out of the soil — is the seed bank's job, and the bank fires rarely by design.

The real risk is legibility. Suppression is a rate change, and rate changes are nearly invisible; the recovery window and the colour shift are doing all the perceptual work. If a player cannot tell a stalled plant from a healthy one, the whole mechanic reads as "sometimes the plants change", which is indistinguishable from noise. Before this ships, the thing to prototype is not the ecology — this bench says the ecology works — it is the visual language of suppression.

How to read this

The grid. One pixel per cell, 4096 cells, wrapped as a torus so there are no edges — an island would have edge effects and we want the disturbance signal clean. Colour is species identity. Dimmed pixels are suppressed occupants (peaceful mode only): still alive, not reproducing, takeable by anything. Empty cells take the page's sunk colour. Rings mark disturbances from the last 40 ticks, fading with age; a dashed ring marks the patch being traced below.

The species. Rank 0 is the best competitor and always displaces anyone it reaches. Rank n−1 is the worst competitor and the best coloniser. Colonisation rates are laid out geometrically from 0.02 to 0.55 and then assigned to ranks by interpolating, in log space, between a perfectly ordered assignment (trade-off 1) and a shuffled one (trade-off 0). That matters: at trade-off 0 the set of colonisation rates is unchanged, only their correlation with rank is destroyed, so the control isolates the trade-off rather than also changing how fast things spread. The rail reports the realised rank–log(c) correlation so you can see what the dial actually did.

Disturbance rate and radius are deliberately separated. D is the expected fraction of the whole grid disturbed per tick; the number of events fired is D · N / πR². So raising R makes disturbance patchier without making it more severe overall, which is the comparison you want. R and D confounded would make the sweep meaningless.

Lethal vs peaceful. Lethal empties the disc. Peaceful leaves every occupant alive but sets a recovery countdown during which it cannot reproduce and can be displaced by anything, regardless of rank. Note what peaceful does not abolish: displacement. One plant still eventually replaces another, cell by cell, continuously — that is succession, and players read it as growth. What it abolishes is the event: eighty plants vanishing at once, which is unambiguously a die-off no matter how you draw it.

The seed bank is two slots per cell holding the two most recent distinct occupants, each with a deposit time; viability decays as 0.5^(age / half-life). Cells deposit when their occupant is displaced, dies of background mortality, or is caught by a disturbance. On disturbance the bank germinates before the current occupant's own propagule lands, which is the only reason the bank is a memory of the past rather than an echo of the present — get that ordering wrong and the mechanic silently does nothing. A germination that introduces a species currently at zero cells is counted as a revival, and since a species at zero cells has no other possible source, revivals are exactly the thing the bank buys.

The hump test. The bench does not eyeball the curve. For each series it takes the argmax, requires it to be interior (not at either end), and requires it to clear both endpoints by 1.96 × the pooled standard error. Only then does it print "hump". Otherwise it prints "rises", "falls" or "flat". The verdicts under the chart are computed from the data you are looking at, so if you change the parameters and re-run, they will change with it — and if they say flat, the page says flat.

On the palette. Species hues are spread evenly around the OKLCH circle at a constant chroma, so no species reads as more important than another. Lightness alternates between two steps by index parity — not by rank, so it encodes nothing — purely because twelve hues at a single lightness are indistinguishable under deuteranopia: the worst adjacent pair measured ΔE 1.5, and alternating lightness lifts it to about 14. In sRGB the teal region still caps below the ideal separation at twelve species, so the legend carries rank numbers and the grid has a hover readout; identity is never colour alone.
