Markings

Wonder has a colour system and a structure system. It has no system at all for the surface — the spots on a wing, the banding on a petal, the mottling on a critter's flank. Grammars are bad at that; reaction–diffusion is what actually makes it in life. Pretty patterns are the easy part. The question this bench exists to answer is whether a small genetic change gives a small pattern change, because if tiny parameter nudges cause wild jumps then a pattern can never be a lineage's identity, and the whole idea is decoration rather than biology.

3 · What "pattern distance" means

A heritability claim is worth nothing without a real metric, so here is the one this page uses, stated in full. Two settled fields are compared by three numbers, each invariant to where the pattern happens to sit:

  • Scale (weight 0.5). Cosine distance between the radially averaged 2-D power spectra. Answers "how big are the features". Power spectra throw away phase, which is exactly right: a lineage's identity is the size and spacing of its marks, not which pixel a particular spot landed on.
  • Orientation (weight 0.2). The power-weighted axial resultant q = Σ P·(cos 2θ, sin 2θ) / Σ P, compared as a vector. Its length is how directional the pattern is; its angle is which way. Doubling θ is the standard trick for axial data — stripes have no head or tail.
  • Tone (weight 0.3). Total-variation distance between 10-bin histograms of V. This is the term that separates spots from holes, which the power spectrum alone cannot do: invert a pattern and its spectrum is unchanged.

The weights are a judgement call and the page shows the three components separately so you can re-weight them by eye. Two properties are not judgement calls: the same genome run twice with the same seed gives D = 0.00000 exactly, and the metric is bounded in [0, 1].

A distance is meaningless without a scale, so both ends are measured, not assumed. The ceiling is the mean distance between unrelated viable genomes — as different as two patterns in this study ever get. The floor is the distance between two runs of the same genome with a different growth seed: developmental noise, the part of the appearance that no child inherits. Note what the floor is and is not. It is not a lower bound on visible mutation, because on this page children inherit their parent's growth seed and so carry none of that noise. It is the price of not inheriting it — and §7 shows that price is the whole game.

4 · The heritability curve

The crux. Take many viable parents, mutate each by δ — a fraction of every parameter's range, applied as Gaussian noise to F, k and the anisotropy at once — and measure the distance from parent to child. A usable system gives a smooth, monotone curve. A cliff means the (F, k) plane has phase boundaries you fall off. Children inherit the growth seed, which §7 shows is not optional.

Every δ walks the same fifty children further out: one unit-Gaussian direction is drawn per parent–child pair and then merely rescaled by δ. Without that — drawing fresh mutations at each δ — sampling noise is larger than the effect being measured and the curve comes out non-monotone by luck alone. We found that out the hard way; the first version of this sweep failed its own monotonicity check.

The live sweep runs at 32² so it finishes in seconds. That is honest about the shape — the 32² and 64² curves never differ by more than 0.013 in the mean — but it inflates the absolute numbers, because every distance here is a statistic over however many pattern features fit on the grid, and 32² fits only about three. The floor measured on the same ten genomes is 0.117 at 32², 0.066 at 64² and 0.038 at 128². So the live "safe up to" reads about one δ step more conservative than the 64² reference figure quoted in the Findings. Both are printed; neither is hidden.

The mean is not where the cliff shows up — the mean is smooth all the way. The cliff is in the tail and in the death rate: the 90th percentile stays flat and then leaps, at the δ where a typical mutation step is large enough to carry a child across a phase boundary or off the crescent entirely.

6 · At game resolution

A gorgeous field at 64² is worth nothing to Wonder if it turns to mush at sprite scale. Each shape below is drawn twice at exactly the pixel count it would have in the game. The top row crops a window of the field sized so two or three features land inside the sprite. The bottom row crams the whole field into the same pixels — which is what you get if you never think about it.

The number under each sprite is contrast kept: the standard deviation of the box-filtered sprite divided by that of the full field. It is a direct measure of how much of the pattern survived the shrink, and it collapses exactly where Nyquist says it should — once a pattern feature is worth less than about two sprite pixels, box-averaging eats it. The chart below averages over four crop origins, because a single crop can happen to land inside one blob and read as flat; that is a fact about that crop, not about the pattern. The sprites above are one honest crop each, so expect them to scatter around the curve.

7 · The 7×7 identity map

Wonder already has a small pixel grid where a plant presents an appearance and an insect's sensor adapts toward it. If surface pattern is that map rather than a separate decoration, then one thing is simultaneously the beautiful thing and the functional thing. So: reduce the pattern to the map's real size and ask whether anything survives. Top canvas is the cropped field, bottom is the 7×7 the game would actually store. The number is the distance between that tile and the parent's, on z-scored tiles, where 1.13 is what two unrelated random tiles score.

The last cell is the one that decides the design. It is the parent's genome, exactly, grown from a different seed — a sibling, not a mutant. If it scores like a stranger, then the 7×7 tile is not carrying the genome at all, it is carrying the accident of where the first blobs landed, and the identity-map tie-in fails unless the growth seed is itself inherited.

Yes — reaction–diffusion is heritable, and the curve is smoother than the parameter plane has any right to allow. Mean pattern distance rises strictly monotonically across three decades of δ, at both grid sizes, with no step in it, and the 32² and 64² curves never differ by more than 0.013 — so this is a property of the model and not of the grid. At δ = 0.004 a child sits at 10 % of the unrelated-genome ceiling; at δ = 0.008, 16 %; at δ = 0.016, 26 %. Those are family resemblances, and they are stable enough to serve as a lineage's identity.

The cliffs are real, but they live in the tail, not the mean. Up to δ = 0.008 the 90th percentile of D stays at or under the kin threshold of 0.112 — nine children in ten are unmistakably kin. At δ = 0.016 the 90th percentile leaps to 0.249 and at 0.032 to 0.372, past the unrelated-genome ceiling: one child in ten is now a stranger. The map explains exactly why. Pattern occupies 22 % of the plane, 30 % of that is one cell from the dead region, and a δ = 0.016 step is about two-thirds of a map cell — so at that rate a lineage regularly walks off the crescent. The measured death rate agrees: 0 % at δ = 0.002, 6 % at 0.008, 12 % at 0.032, 36 % at 0.128.

The safe mutation range is δ ≤ 0.008 of parameter range per event — in absolute terms, one standard deviation of ΔF ≈ 0.00064 and Δk ≈ 0.00036. That is the largest δ at which nine children in ten stay inside the kin threshold and fewer than one in ten dies. The upper bound is not soft: doubling to δ = 0.016 more than doubles the tail and doubles the deaths.

And here is the uncomfortable coincidence that decides the design. The δ at which a mutation moves a pattern as far as merely regrowing it (D = 0.055) is δ ≈ 0.008 — the same place the safe band ends. There is no comfortable window where the genetic signal is loud and the developmental noise is quiet. Push the mutation rate up to be seen and you fall off the crescent; keep it low and, if the growth seed is re-rolled each generation, the noise is bigger than the inheritance. The only way out is to stop generating noise: inherit the seed. Then parent and child share their initial conditions exactly, the floor does not apply to them at all, and δ = 0.001 already produces a clean visible nudge.

One caveat on the regime labels, because they look worse than the patterns do. 10 % of children change regime label at δ = 0.008, next to a mean D of 0.053 — but the baseline for that is not zero. Regrow the same genome from a different seed and it changes label 11 % of the time, which is to say the label flips as often from noise as from a safe-sized mutation. Most flips are cosmetic crossings of a classifier threshold — spots whose blobs have just elongated enough to be called labyrinth. The label is more brittle than the appearance. Trust D, not the name.

Developmental noise is not a property of the chemistry — it is a property of how small the field is. Run the same ten genomes on three grids and the floor falls monotonically with the number of pattern features you are averaging over: 0.117 at 32² (3.3 features across), 0.066 at 64² (8.0), 0.038 at 128² (14.5). On a large field the same genome always looks the same, because the statistics converge. On a small one it does not, because they don't. This matters because a sprite is a small field — the smallest one on this page. Whatever noise the metric sees at 32² is a floor on what the game will see at 8×8, and it is why §7 comes out the way it does.

Pixel-scale survival is a hard geometric constraint, and it is the finding most likely to change how this ships. The wavelength is set by the chemistry — 4.9 to 8.5 cells, call it 7 — and it does not care how big your sprite is. Only one number matters, field cells per sprite pixel, and contrast retention falls off it on the Nyquist schedule: 100 % at 1, 90 % at 2, 75 % at 3, 59 % at 4, 33 % at 6, 19 % at 12. All five sprite sizes lie on that one curve within four points — a 32×32 sprite and a 7×7 sprite lose the same fraction at the same cells-per-pixel, so sprite size is not the variable, the crop is. Concretely: an 8×8 insect wing showing a 96-cell field is 12 cells per pixel and keeps 19 % — mush. Show it a 16-cell window instead, 2 cells per pixel and 2.3 features, and it keeps 90 % — three clean marks. So the sprite must not show the whole field. Crop deliberately, aim for two to three field cells per sprite pixel, and accept what that implies: an 8×8 wing carries two or three marks and no more; a 7×7 identity map carries about two features, which is the difference between "two dots", "one bar" and "plain", and nothing finer.

The identity-map tie-in works, but only if the growth seed becomes part of the genome. This is the sharpest result on the page. With the seed inherited, a δ = 0.002 child's 7×7 tile sits at 0.53 against a random baseline of 1.13, and degrades gracefully to 0.71 at δ = 0.008. With the seed re-rolled, a sibling with an identical genome scores 1.14 — indistinguishable from two random tiles, and further from its parent than an unrelated genome is (1.06). At sprite scale the literal pixels are dominated by where the first blobs happened to land, not by the chemistry. Inherit the seed and the tile is heritable; don't, and an insect adapted to the parent will not recognise the child. It costs one extra integer in the genome.

Two parameters must stay locked, and the numbers say how hard. Du/Dv is not a fourth free dial: at ratio 1.5 only 10 % of the plane is viable, at 2.0 it is 22 %, at 3.0 it is 44 %. Moving it rescales the whole map and invalidates every δ on this page. Anisotropy is safer and is the only way to get aligned stripes at all — isotropic Gray–Scott makes labyrinths, never stripes, and the map bears that out with 4 stripe cells out of 351 at α = 0. Push α to 0.6 and 84 % of viable cells become stripes while the viable area grows by 43 %. Anisotropy is a cheap, safe, expressive dial. The diffusion ratio is not.

One methodological finding worth writing down, because it cost an hour. How you seed the field decides which half of the map exists. Perturbing scattered single cells loses the entire high-F region — coral, κ and μ all decay to uniform — because there the patterned state is bistable with the trivial one and needs a nucleus above a critical size. Seeding with small square blobs recovers all of it. A "which regimes exist" claim is a claim about the initial conditions as much as about (F, k).

How to read this

What the model is. Two chemicals on a torus. U is fed in at rate F and V is drained at rate F + k; the reaction U + 2V → 3V converts one to the other, so V is autocatalytic — it needs itself to make more of itself. V diffuses at half U's rate, and that difference is the whole engine: the activator stays put while the inhibitor spreads, which is Turing's 1952 argument in its smallest possible form. Everything on this page falls out of those two lines of arithmetic run a few thousand times.

Read the map first. Horizontal is F, vertical is k, and the grey mass is death — the trivial state wins and nothing happens. Pattern is the crescent. Inside it, spots sit toward high k, gaps and labyrinths toward low k, and the ragged tail at low F and low k never settles at all. The white ring is the current genome. Everything else on the page is a question about how far you can move that ring before the thing inside it stops being the same thing.

Read the curve second. Two horizontal reference lines matter more than the curve itself. The lower one is developmental noise — how different the same genome looks when grown twice. The upper one is how different two unrelated genomes look. Heritability is the claim that mutation distance rises smoothly between those two lines as δ grows, and that is what the curve does. The shaded band is the range of δ where children are reliably kin; the bar chart underneath is the price you pay in dead children for going past it.

Why the metric ignores where the spots are. Two plants of the same lineage grown side by side have their spots in different places, and nobody would call them different species for it. So the texture metric works on power spectra and histograms, which are blind to position by construction. That is the right choice for "is this the same kind of pattern" — and it is the wrong choice for §7, where the question is whether two literal 7×7 grids match. Section 7 therefore uses a different, position-sensitive measure, and gets a different and much harsher answer. Both are true; they are answering different questions.

What this does and does not tell us about Wonder. It tells us surface pattern can be genetic: two floats, an anisotropy term and an integer seed — about ten bytes — give a heritable, mutable, continuously-varying marking with a measured safe mutation rate. It is cheap in the way that matters, too, because it is computed once at birth and baked into a tiny tile; nothing here runs per tick. It tells us the tile has to be small and the crop has to be deliberate. It does not tell us the pattern means anything. Nothing here selects; a spotted plant is no fitter than a plain one, and the ecology audit's finding that Wonder has no fitness function is untouched by this bench. And it does not tell us the 7×7 map should be a pattern at all — only that if it is, it can be, and what it costs.

What the player would actually see. A stand of the same plant, every one of them wearing the same markings — the same size of spot, the same crowding, the same slant — and wearing them in the same places, generation after generation, because the growth seed is inherited along with the chemistry. Then a seedling comes up with its spots a little larger and a little further apart. Not a different plant: the same plant, marked differently, and you can see the family in it. Roughly one seedling in twelve at the recommended rate comes up plain — no markings at all, because its parameters fell off the edge of the crescent — and that one is a dead end rather than a variant, which is worth knowing before a player names it. At the scale the game actually draws things, an insect's wing carries two marks and a petal carries three or four; anything finer is a smudge, and the honest version of this feature is bold, coarse markings that a player can read at a glance rather than fine mottling nobody will ever resolve.
