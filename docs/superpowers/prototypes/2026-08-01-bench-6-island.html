The island

Five sibling benches each test one mechanism in isolation. This one puts the reagent economy on a grid and lets it compete: soil with a tiled motif, roots grown once at germination, a metabolic chain run once at maturity, and two lines of code — reproduction × vigor and the crowding thin picks the weakest of three — that turn a drifting population into a selected one. The question it exists to answer is narrow: does selection visibly beat drift, and can you see it happening?

How to read this

The ground is a tiled motif, not noise, and that is the whole point. Every base reagent has a small P×P pattern rolled per world per biome, repeated across the biome and then multiplied by a slow moisture gradient. A pattern that repeats is a pattern a root can be shaped to fit; pure noise has no stable target, so roots can only ever be generalists and the system flattens into drift with extra steps. Pattern regularity morphs the field continuously between the two, and finding where adaptation stops working was one of this bench's two jobs. It turned out not to — see the findings; the sweep button reproduces the whole table, including the field statistics that show the slider is doing what it claims.

A plant computes twice in its life and never again. At germination it grows a root — cells placed one at a time, each going to the tile with the highest marginal uptake, so the shape of the root is an argument about where the food is. That mask is cached forever. At maturity it runs its hand of conversion cards once against whatever the roots pulled up, and caches the energy, the pigment and the body that come out. Every tick after that is a table read. This is the event-driven rule from §15, and it is what makes a few thousand plants affordable.

Mobile and immobile reagents demand opposite roots. Taken from OpenSimRoot: a mobile reagent diffuses, so its drawdown spreads to neighbours and a tile's supply is captured almost fully by any root that touches it — 1 − e^(−1.5m). Piling more mass into that tile is wasted; reaching another tile is not. An immobile reagent forms a tight depletion halo and is captured in proportion to root mass in contact — 0.5m, linear — so the correct answer is to pack mass onto the richest tile you can find. One flag, two correct answers. Worth knowing before you read the charts: both shapes are phenotypic. They fall out of the growth rule on contact with the ground, so they appear at selection strength 0 too. What selection decides is which of the two ends up owning the island.

Vigor is a z-score, so selection strength means what it says. surplus = chain energy − (rootCost × cells + upkeep), and vigor = exp(strength × z × 0.5) where z is that plant's surplus standardised against the living population. At strength 0 every vigor is exactly 1.0 and both fitness lines go inert — reproduction is flat and the thin's three-way sample is a coin toss. That is the control, and it is a real one: it is not a different simulation, it is the same simulation with the multiplier removed.

Pollinators close the loop and are deliberately thin. Two swarms carry a sensor hue and a tolerance; a plant whose pigment falls inside a swarm's window gets its reproduction multiplied. Swarms drift their sensor toward the hue they are actually visiting, which is a positive feedback — this is the mechanism most likely to turn a narrowing ribbon into a splitting one, and it is worth watching for that specifically.

The sampling budget is honest, not a cheat. Each tick samples min(480, population) plants for reproduction, mortality and thinning — the same budget flora.ts uses against ~8000 live plants in the shipped game. A consequence worth naming: per-plant reproduction opportunity falls as the population grows, which damps the population on its own before the per-tile cap is reached. Field maintenance (drawdown, regen, diffusion) is full-grid every tick and is the cheaper half.

Findings

All numbers below were measured off this page, not estimated. Seed 2438, default parameters, selection strength 1.4, 900 ticks per arm unless stated. Every one is reproducible with the two buttons above.

Does selection visibly beat drift?

Yes. The drift arm ends at 8.66 effective hue bins with a single broad mode; the selection arm ends at 4.54 with two modes. That is a 48% collapse in hue diversity, and it arrives as a split rather than a single winner — two swarms, two pigment bands. It holds on every seed I tried: 8.7→4.5, 8.8→5.9, 8.6→6.2, 17.8→7.2, a mean of 11.0→6.0.

The ribbons are distinguishable from about tick 250 and unmistakable by tick 400. Before tick 150 they are the same picture, which is the uncomfortable and correct answer: someone watching for thirty seconds sees nothing. Mean surplus is the far better instrument — it separates by tick 50 (−6.1 vs −1.8) and is decisive by tick 250 (−6.0 vs +3.2), ending at −6.0 vs +27.2. Mean tier is nearly as good: 1.07 vs 2.31. The drift arm's tier of 1.07 is worth staring at — with no selection, hands rot under mutation until the whole island is living off the "tier-1 is always edible" floor and nothing metabolises at all.

The spatial half of the loop moves too, and separately: uptake per root cell goes 0.184 → 0.260, +42%. That is selection improving foraging, measured independently of the chemistry.

One caveat that matters more than any of the above. Across four seeds the end-of-run surplus gap was +33.2, +2.7, +2.9, +17.2. Two worlds in four never got a working metabolism going at all — seeds 7 and 42 end with mean tier below 1.6 and mean surplus still negative, even at full selection. The rolled chemistry deck matters more than any slider on the rail, which is exactly the viability problem §4.4 and §12.2 predicted, showing up on its own. A shipped version needs the rejection sampler.

At what pattern regularity does adaptation stop working?

It doesn't. At any value. This is the bench's clearest negative result and it falsifies a design claim I was fairly confident about.

The slider works — measured on the field itself, same-biome correlation at lag P falls 0.97 → 0.94 → 0.84 → 0.67 → 0.54 → 0.48 as regularity goes 1.00 → 0.00, so the motif is genuinely being dissolved into noise. The outcome does not care. Surplus gap across the same six settings: +25.3, +16.0, +20.9, +20.0, +18.8, +22.6 — a scatter with no trend. Uptake-per-cell gain: 47%, 37%, 44%, 46%, 47%, 32% — likewise. Evolved root spread is ≈2.4 tiles at every setting.

The reason is visible in the other field statistic. Lag-1 correlation only falls 0.69 → 0.48, because the large-scale moisture gradient and the per-biome composition survive the slider untouched — and that is where the learnable signal actually lives. On top of that, roots here are grown by tropism at germination (§4.1's recommended option B), which is a plastic process: a root finds the good tiles by looking, so it never needed the pattern to repeat. §4.2's claim that "pattern regularity is the master tuning knob of this entire design" appears to be true only for genotype-fixed roots — a CA rule table, say. Choose tropism growth and you buy plasticity, and plasticity makes the motif decorative. That is a real fork, and it should be decided deliberately rather than discovered in week three.

Did the mobile/immobile split produce two root strategies?

Yes for concentration, no for reach, and not for the reason I expected. Measured at tick 300 across six worlds, selection on: immobile-led plants pack a mean peak tile mass of 4.44 against 2.05 for mobile-led — a clean 2.2×, present in every world. Root spread barely moves: 2.13 vs 1.78 tiles, only 1.2×, because reach is a gene under weak selection while concentration falls straight out of the marginal-uptake rule.

The surprise: the split is phenotypic, not evolved. The drift arm shows the same divergence — 4.65 vs 2.59 peak mass at selection strength 0. Two root shapes exist because the growth rule produces them on contact with the ground, not because anything was selected. What selection changes is which class owns the island: on seed 2438 the mobile/immobile population split goes from 293 / 420 under drift to 967 / 62 under selection at the same tick, and to 1226 / 12 by tick 900. Selection did not invent the strategies; it held an election.

What else surprised me

Selection makes the island emptier before it makes it fuller. The selected arm runs up to 22% below the drift arm through the first ~300 ticks (tick 150: 1324 vs 1707 plants) and only crosses over around tick 340, ending slightly ahead (2045 vs 1993). The thin removes weak plants faster than strong ones can breed. It is a good trough, but "selection on" and "more life" are not the same thing, and a player toggling this would first watch their island thin out.

Selection collapses speciation. Lineages at tick 900: 560 under drift, 173 under selection — a 3.2× reduction, with the population slightly larger. When there is a right answer, load-bearing traits stop wandering and the ✧ split stops firing. That is a straightforward consequence of weighting driftDistance by load-bearing-ness (§6.4) and it means the shipped speciation rate will change the moment vigor lands, on every existing seed.

Catalysts produced a spatial effect I did not design. A catalyst must be present and is not spent, and a plant's starting pile comes only from its own roots — so a catalyse card can only ever fire where the ground actually offers the catalyst. Catalyse-carrying lineages end up pinned to particular biome bands while upgrade-only lineages spread everywhere. Nothing in the code says "range"; it fell out of the soil map.

The floor rule was not optional. The first working build had no "tier-1 is always edible" rule, and a single bad card swap left a plant with an empty pile, zero energy and a fallback pigment. The whole population collapsed onto one hue and the ribbon went mute in both arms — the bench measured nothing for an afternoon. §12.2's first guard is load-bearing, and it is load-bearing for legibility as much as for viability.

What this does and does not tell us

It tells us the loop coheres end to end: soil → root → pile → chain → energy → surplus → descendants, with a readable number at every join and two lines of code carrying the whole rewire. It tells us the signal beats drift within a few hundred ticks at the real sampling budget, that mean surplus and mean tier are much better instruments than the hue ribbon, and that the mobile/immobile flag earns its keep even though it works differently than advertised.

It does not tell us the shipped game behaves this way. This bench has 4 base reagents, 4 biomes, one hand-tuned deck and no plant forms; Wonder has 14 forms, habitat rules, weather multipliers, player tending and a save format, all of which multiply into the same two lines and could easily swamp the vigor term. There is no shade, no seasons, no fauna beyond a two-swarm cartoon, and the pollinator layer is deliberately too thin to trust. Half of the rolled worlds here are duds, so the viability sweep (Demo 5) is not optional. And the speciation numbers are an artefact of my driftDistance weighting, not evidence about the right threshold.

What a player would see. Not the ribbon. They would see one corner of the island go one colour over a few minutes and another corner go another; a stand thinning after it exhausted its own band, then a differently-coloured lineage moving in; and, if they clicked a plant, its roots drawn over the dirt it is eating with the run log spelling the chemistry out in a sentence. The honest risk is the first two hundred ticks, where selection and drift are indistinguishable and the island just looks busy — and the honest opportunity is that "this one is doing better than that one, and here is the picture of why" is something Wonder has never once been able to show.
