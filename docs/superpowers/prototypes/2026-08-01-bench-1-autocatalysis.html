When is a rolled chemistry actually alive?

Every Wonder island rolls its own reagents and its own conversion reactions. Most such rolls are dead: a heap of recipes nobody can start. Kauffman's autocatalytic set gives a principled test for the difference — a sub-network that builds every one of its own ingredients out of freely available food, and catalyses every one of its own steps using only molecules it makes. This bench implements the RAF algorithm of Hordijk & Steel exactly, and then asks the only question worldgen cares about: where is the line, and can we put a gate on it?

The system

A chemistry is generated at random and then tested for one property: whether some part of it can build and catalyse itself out of freely available food. The test is Hordijk & Steel's RAF algorithm, which is a shrinking fixed point — it repeatedly deletes any reaction that cannot be fed or cannot be catalysed, until nothing more can be deleted. What remains is the world's metabolism, or nothing.

Parts

molecule
A binary string of length 1 to L. The molecule set is every such string, so it is 2L+1 − 2 molecules — 14 at L=3, 254 at L=7.
food set F
Every molecule of length ≤ Lf. Food is assumed present in unlimited supply and free. This assumption is what makes the trivial one-reaction RAF possible, and is the reason the naive test has to be rejected.
reaction
Ligation only: a + b → ab, one reaction per ordered pair whose concatenation is still ≤ L. The count grows as Σ(k−1)2k — 20 reactions at L=3, 1284 at L=7. Cleavage is not implemented, which makes every number here a conservative floor.
catalysis
Independently for each (molecule, reaction) pair, that molecule catalyses that reaction with probability p. No rates and no strengths — a molecule either catalyses a reaction or it does not.
f
p × |R|: the mean number of reactions a single molecule catalyses. This, not p, is the quantity the theory is about, and it is the top axis of the sweep chart.
closure(F, R)
Everything reachable from the food set using only reactions in R. Computed by repeated application until no new molecule appears.
RA
Reflexively autocatalytic: every reaction in the set is catalysed by something the set touches. Says nothing about whether the set can ever start.
F-generated
Every reactant in the set is reachable from food. Says nothing about whether anything catalyses it. Converges in a single pass.
RAF
Both at once, and the reason the two have to be applied in alternation: shrinking the set shrinks its own catalytic support, so the conditions have to be re-checked against each other until they stop changing.
adjacent possible
Every reaction exactly one step beyond the live metabolism's closure — reactants already on hand, product not yet made. Split into latent (the reaction was rolled, it just has not been reached) and novel (this world does not contain that reaction at all).

Procedure

  1. Enumerate every molecule of length ≤ L; mark those of length ≤ Lf as food.
  2. Enumerate every ligation reaction. Optionally keep a random sample of them, which thins the network without changing its shape.
  3. Roll catalysis: each molecule catalyses each reaction with probability p.
  4. Run the fixed point. Start with every reaction; delete any whose reactants are not in the closure or whose catalysts are all outside it; recompute the closure; repeat until stable. The result is the unique maximal RAF — there is no search and no tuning, one answer per world.
  5. Apply the gate: |maxRAF| ≥ 5 reactions. The naive "does any RAF exist" test is reported alongside it, dashed, so the gap is visible.
  6. Compute the adjacent possible from the closure of the surviving set.
  7. For the sweep: 40 values of p × 25 independent worlds each, plotting the fraction that pass.

The phase transition

The money chart. Forty catalysis probabilities, twenty-five independent worlds each, plotted as the fraction that pass. Kauffman's claim is that this transition is sharp — that life is not a matter of degree but of crossing a line. The upper axis is the quantity the theory is actually about: f, the mean number of reactions a single molecule catalyses; the shaded band marks the order-1 region (f ≈ 1–2) that both Kauffman and Hordijk & Steel report for the binary polymer model. It is a reference drawn from the literature, not a fit to our data. Two curves, because the naive test lies: the dashed one is "does any RAF exist", and it is contaminated by one-reaction RAFs of the form food + food → x, catalysed by food, which satisfy the definition and describe a dead island. The solid one requires the RAF to be at least five reactions, and it is the one worth gating on.

How to read this

A RAF, in one sentence: a set of reactions that makes all of its own ingredients starting from food, and catalyses all of its own steps using only things it makes. That is the whole idea. Everything below is bookkeeping.

The algorithm is a shrinking fixed point, and it is short enough to read: 

R' = every reaction this world rolled
loop:
  W   = closure(F, R')          molecules reachable from food using R'
  R'' = { r in R' : every reactant of r is in W
                    AND at least one catalyst of r is in W }
  if R'' == R' : stop
  R' = R''
maxRAF = R'   (empty = no RAF exists)

It only ever removes reactions, so it terminates, and it returns the unique maximal RAF: if any RAF exists at all, this finds one, and every other RAF is a subset of it. That uniqueness is what makes it usable as a gate — there is no search, no tuning, one answer per world.

The two weaker conditions are shown separately because they fail differently. RA (reflexively autocatalytic) asks only that every reaction be catalysed by something the set touches; it will happily certify a set that can never be started, because nothing in it is reachable from food. F-generated asks only that everything be buildable from food; it will certify a set that no one is catalysing, i.e. a list of reactions that in principle could happen and in practice never do. Note the F-generated maximum converges in a single pass — dropping a reaction that never fired cannot change what the closure reached — while RA has to iterate, because shrinking the set shrinks its own support. RAF is contained in both maxima and is normally strictly smaller than their intersection. When you see that gap in the bars, that is exactly the work the alternation is doing.

A RAF can be real and still worthless, and this is the trap. The food set is unlimited by assumption, so a single reaction 0 + 1 → 01 that happens to be catalysed by a food molecule is, formally, a maximal RAF: its one reactant pair is in the closure, its one catalyst is in the closure, it is self-sustaining. It is also nothing — a world where one reaction fires and no metabolism exists. At the network sizes we can afford to compute, these trivial RAFs are not a curiosity, they dominate: at L=5 the naive "any RAF" test is already passing half the time at f ≈ 0.5, an order of magnitude below where anything resembling a metabolism appears. So the bench reports and gates on maxRAF ≥ 5 reactions. The threshold barely matters, because the RAF is violently bimodal: it is either nothing, or it is most of the reaction set. At L=6 the median maxRAF goes from 107 reactions to 234 across a single 1.34× step in p; at L=7 it goes from 0 to 520 across the same step. There is no gentle middle to be sensitive to.

Where the transition sits, in the parameters we actually intend to use. Read the top axis first. In a ligation system up to length L the reaction count grows as Σ(k−1)2k — 20 reactions at L=3, 196 at L=5, 1284 at L=7. Across that range the critical probability p falls by 12× while f = p × |R| rises by only 5×, and it rises linearly in L (roughly +0.65 per unit of length) while |R| rises exponentially. That is the shape of Hordijk & Steel's correction to Kauffman: the catalytic burden per molecule needed for life grows slowly, not explosively, with system size. But be honest about the size of the effect at our scale — over L = 3…7, f is the more stable of the two dials and it is nowhere near constant. The practical rule is not "set f = 1.5". It is "measure f₀.₅ once for the L you shipped, then place the world relative to it." That measurement is what this bench is.

Shrinking the reaction sample below 1.0 moves both numbers at once: it cuts |R| so f falls for a fixed p, and it thins the network so the food closure gets patchy. Those are two different deaths and the readout tells them apart. Holding f fixed at 2.0 and thinning at L=5: at half the reactions, one rolled reaction in five is already unreachable from food and the closure has dropped from 62 molecules to 52; at a quarter, half the reactions are unreachable and the closure is down to 28. The RAF itself survives surprisingly far — worlds still pass at 15% of reactions — they are just small. Thinning does not kill the metabolism, it shrinks the world.

What this does and does not tell us about Wonder. It tells us there is a real, computable, non-arbitrary line between a chemistry that can bootstrap and one that cannot; that the line is sharp enough to gate on; and that the naive form of the test — "does a RAF exist" — is not the one to ship. It replaces the ad-hoc viability checks currently in worldgen with one test that has a proof behind it, is parameter-free given the chemistry, and returns a unique answer. It also hands us a free richness dial: distance above the transition is a one-dimensional measure of how metabolically deep an island is, and it is measurable before a single tile is drawn. Concretely, the gate would be: roll the chemistry, run maxRAF, require |maxRAF| ≥ 5 and |closure(F, maxRAF)| ≥ |F| + k, reroll otherwise — and set the catalysis density from a one-off sweep at the L we ship, not from a guessed constant.

It does not tell us the island will be interesting. The honest simplifications: ligation only (the cleavage twin ab → a + b exists in the full binary polymer model and is not implemented here, which makes these numbers a conservative floor — adding cleavage roughly doubles |R| and shifts p down accordingly); no concentrations and no kinetics, so a RAF here says a metabolism is possible, not that it runs at a useful rate or that it survives a competitor; catalysis is binary, present or absent, not a rate multiplier, so nothing distinguishes a weak catalyst from a strong one; and food is unlimited, which is exactly the assumption a real ecology gets to violate. A RAF is a necessary condition for a living chemistry, not a sufficient one.

What a player would ever see of this: nothing, directly. No player of Wonder will ever look at a reaction graph, and we should not build one for them. What they see is that some islands are metabolically rich and some are thin — that one shore is dense with plants running long chains and strange pigments, and another is three species of scrub in two colours all the way to the horizon. That difference is a thing you feel in twenty seconds of walking, and this is the mechanism that decides which one worldgen rolled. The adjacent possible is the other player-facing half: it is how an island gets to invent something later in a run without a designer having written the recipe, and the player experiences that as a shore that keeps producing new things long after they thought they had seen it.

Findings

Sweeping p across five decades, 40 values × 25 independent worlds per value, reaction sample 1.0, food set Lf = 2, seed 3141. Substantive = maxRAF of at least five reactions.

L = 3
20 reactions. p₀.₅ ≈ 2.9×10−2 → f ≈ 0.58. Naive test crosses at f ≈ 0.10.
L = 4
68 reactions. p₀.₅ ≈ 1.1×10−2 → f ≈ 0.76. Naive: f ≈ 0.15.
L = 5
196 reactions. p₀.₅ ≈ 7.6×10−3 → f ≈ 1.48. Naive: f ≈ 0.49. 10% of worlds pass at f = 0.71, 90% at f = 2.14.
L = 6
516 reactions. p₀.₅ ≈ 4.8×10−3 → f ≈ 2.45. Naive: f ≈ 1.55.
L = 7
1284 reactions. p₀.₅ ≈ 2.4×10−3 → f ≈ 3.08. Naive: f ≈ 2.80.

The headline. Across L = 3…7 the reaction count grows 64× and the critical probability p falls 12×, while critical f rises only 5× — and it rises about linearly in L (≈ +0.65 per unit of length) against an exponentially growing |R|. That is the predicted shape. But it is not the clean invariant a summary of the literature suggests: f at the transition runs from 0.58 to 3.08 over this range, straddling rather than sitting inside the reference f = 1–2 band. Two reasons, both ours: this model is ligation only, which removes the single-reactant cleavage reactions that are the cheapest way to satisfy the F-generated condition; and L ≤ 7 is well below the n = 10–20 at which the published measurements were taken. So: the theory's shape reproduces, its constant does not transfer, and we must measure our own.

Sharpness — sharper than expected, once you stop counting trivia. The width of the substantive transition (p at 90% over p at 10%) narrows from 3.7× at L=3 to 3.0× at L=5 to 1.6× at L=7. Underneath it the maxRAF is starkly bimodal: at L=6 the median jumps 107→234 reactions across one 1.34× step in p, and at L=7 it jumps 0→520 across the same step. A giant autocatalytic component is born all at once. This is why the ≥5-reaction threshold is not a fudge factor — anywhere between 3 and 50 would draw the same curve.

The naive test is unusable, and that is the single most actionable result here. "Does a RAF exist" crosses 50% at f ≈ 0.49 at L=5 while a real metabolism needs f ≈ 1.48 — a 3× error, all of it one-reaction RAFs of the form food + food → x catalysed by food. The gap closes as L grows (at L=7 the two crossings are 2.80 vs 3.08) but at any world size we would plausibly ship it is large enough to pass islands that are visibly dead.

The default state is the trap, on purpose. Seed 3141, L=5, p = 10−3 (f ≈ 0.20) has a maxRAF of exactly one reaction. It passes the letter of Kauffman's test. Its closure is 7 molecules of a possible 62 — the six food molecules and one more. Press Jump p to the transition: p goes to ≈7.6×10−3, maxRAF becomes 17 reactions, the closure becomes 20 molecules, and the adjacent possible widens from 30 to 37. That is also the clearest view of why the algorithm has to iterate: at that point maxRA = 74 reactions and maxF-generated = 196, their intersection is 74, and the true maxRAF is 17. The alternation throws away 57 reactions that pass both conditions separately.

Islands near the line have the most room to invent. At L=5, mean adjacent-possible size peaks at f ≈ 1.5 (32.5 molecules, with a live 10-reaction RAF and a closure of 15) and collapses to 2.6 by f = 3.5 and to zero by f = 6, where the closure is the entire molecule set and there is nothing left to discover. As a ratio of frontier to molecules-actually-made it falls monotonically: 3.6 at f = 1.5, 0.82 at f = 2.0, 0.05 at f = 3.5. If we want islands that keep producing new things long into a run, we want them just above the line — which is the opposite of what a "more chemistry is better" gate would select.

Where it does not go. L = 3 is too small to be interesting (20 reactions, and the whole molecule set is 14 strings), and L = 7 at full sample is 1284 reactions — comfortably fast here, but each world in the sweep is a full RAF fixed point and 1000 of them take a couple of seconds even chunked. Worldgen only needs one per island, so cost is a non-issue in the game; it is only a bench issue.
