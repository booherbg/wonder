Wonder · ecology foundations · bench 5 · working prototype

Trophic flow: fund the individuals, or move the ceiling?

Two decisions about how energy climbs Wonder's food chain, settled with numbers instead of argument. First: should each level's population be paid for out of the level below at a realistic 10% transfer, or should the level below merely set a soft ceiling that the level above relaxes toward? Second: five of Wonder's clocks already tick — day, tide, rain, bloom, aurora — and none of them touches carrying capacity. If periodically wobbling capacity widens coexistence, that is close to free. This bench is a single well-mixed patch: no space, no geography, no dispersal.

The system

One well-mixed patch — no space, no geography, no dispersal — holding a four-level food chain: soil, several competing producer species, pollinators, birds. The same chain is then simulated two ways that differ only in how energy couples one level to the next, on identical seeds, so the difference in outcome is attributable to the coupling and to nothing else. A second experiment wobbles the soil supply periodically and counts how many species survive.

Parts

level
Soil → producers → pollinators → birds. Only the producer level has multiple competing species; the levels above it are aggregate.
funded coupling
The realistic model. Each level's growth is paid for out of the level below at a 10% transfer efficiency, and every individual must earn back an upkeep m each tick or the level shrinks.
ceiling coupling
The alternative. The level below sets a soft carrying capacity, and the level above relaxes toward it at rate r instead of being funded by it. No energy is conserved between levels; the lower level constrains rather than pays.
competition α
The weight a producer species places on everyone else's density relative to its own. At α = 0 each species has a private niche; past about 1 the strongest competitor takes everything.
demographic noise σ
Per-tick stochastic wobble scaling as √n, so small populations feel it hardest. Set it to zero to confirm that funded-mode collapse is a limit cycle rather than bad luck.
periodic forcing
S(t) = S₀ · (1 + A·sin(2πt/T)) on the soil supply. Wonder already has five clocks — day, tide, rain, bloom, aurora — spanning roughly this range of periods, none of them touching carrying capacity.
phase spread
Not in the base model, added as a test. At 1, each species' capacity peaks at a different point in the cycle — the obvious way to build a temporal niche.
coexistence
The count of producer species still above an extinction floor at the end of the run. Competitive exclusion is slow, so short runs flatter it.

Procedure

  1. Roll nP producer species, each with its own uptake efficiency from the seed, so their capacities genuinely differ.
  2. Simulate the chain for the full run length under the selected coupling, applying competition, noise, and any periodic forcing each tick.
  3. Decision 1. Run the same seed under both couplings and compare persistence of the top level across 200 paired seeds.
  4. Decision 2. Sweep forcing amplitude A against period T, counting coexisting species in each cell, and difference it against the A = 0 control row — the phase diagram is that difference, so a positive cell means forcing actually bought something.

Decision 1 · the same seed, run both ways

Identical seed, identical producer noise draws, identical consumption terms — the only difference is the rule that sets animal numbers. Funded mode grows each level by n · (e·intake − upkeep). Ceiling mode relaxes each level toward K = e · (biomass below) at rate r. Both get the same tiny propagule floor, so neither is ever permanently dead; the question is only how much of the time the top level is actually there.

Decision 1 — the ceiling wins, and it is not close. Paired difference in top-level persistence +0.510 (SE 0.0085, t = 60): the ceiling model keeps a top level present 100% of ticks, the funded model 49%. Funded mode blinks out and back roughly 20 times per thousand ticks. Sweeping upkeep from 0.01 to 0.14 never rescues it — persistence runs 0.81 → 0.04 and falls monotonically. The only place funded mode becomes reliable is transfer efficiency e ≥ 0.35 (persistence 0.95 at 0.35, 1.00 at 0.50) — i.e. only if we abandon the realistic 10% and make the food chain physically implausible. Note also the last two rows of the table: the funded chain does not even deliver the pyramid it was supposed to justify. It realises 3.0% at the top step because the population it is funding spends half its life in the refuge.

Why funded mode fails is worth understanding, because it is not the noise. Set demographic noise to zero and the funded chain still collapses: the deterministic trajectory is a Rosenzweig–MacArthur limit cycle whose top-level trough reaches 0.003 against a threshold of 0.030. A Holling type II consumer stacked two deep oscillates — that is textbook, not a tuning accident — and once the trough is under the threshold, a bounded world reads it as death. Demographic noise then makes it worse in exactly the way small numbers always do: fluctuations scale as √n, so at a nominal standing crop of 1 the top level's relative noise is 10× the producers'.

Decision 2 — negative result. Periodic forcing does not widen coexistence here; it very slightly narrows it. Summed over the whole competition-strength grid, mean surviving species falls from 32.55 at A = 0 to 31.06 at A = 0.9 — −4.6%. Every cell in the grid is zero or negative; the worst is −0.40 species at α = 0.30. The competitive boundary — the largest α at which two species still coexist on average — moves from about 0.80 down to about 0.76. For the top level, forcing does nothing at all: paired difference in persistence −0.004 ± 0.006, t = −0.6, across periods from 30 to 400 ticks.

And the null is structural, not empirical. Forcing multiplies every species' capacity by the same S(t), so every ratio Ki/Kj is unchanged — and the Lotka–Volterra coexistence condition depends only on those ratios. There is no channel through which shared multiplicative forcing could create a temporal niche. What is left is the second-order cost: the trough of each cycle pushes rare species nearer the threshold, and some of them do not come back. That is the −4.6%.

The obvious repair makes it dramatically worse, which is the useful part. Turn up niche phase spread and species stop sharing a phase — each one's capacity peaks at a different point in the cycle, which is what a temporal niche is supposed to look like. Surviving species at α = 0.3 then go 3.88 → 3.50 → 2.00 → 1.00 as amplitude rises 0 → 0.3 → 0.6 → 0.9. You can reproduce that on this page: drag the slider to 1 and recompute, and the grid's top row falls from 43.2 species to 18.6 — −56.9% where the shared-phase version managed −4.6%. Asynchronous forcing without a storage mechanism is not a niche, it is a periodic execution: whoever is in their trough gets squeezed by everyone in their peak, and nothing in this model buffers them through it. Chesson's storage effect needs a life stage that survives the bad season — a seed bank, a dormant egg, a rhizome. This bench has none, so it collects the cost of variation and none of the benefit. If Wonder wants periodic forcing to buy diversity, the thing to build is not the wobble; it is the seed bank.

Swailem & Täuber's result stands; it is this bench's reach that is limited. Their system is a stochastic lattice with local, discrete individuals, and their coexistence region is predator–prey, not multi-species competitive. A single well-mixed patch with continuous densities is structurally blind to anything that needs space or discreteness to work. So the honest conclusion is not "the effect is not real" — it is "we cannot claim it for free; it would have to be re-tested on a spatial bench, and this one gives no reason to expect it to survive the trip."

How to read this

The model is one patch, stepped discretely. Soil flux is S(t) = S₀·(1 + A·sin(2πt/T)). Each producer species i has its own uptake efficiency uᵢ drawn from the seed, giving it capacity Kᵢ(t) = c·S(t)·uᵢ, and they compete Lotka–Volterra style through a shared competition coefficient α: a species feels its own density at full weight and everyone else's at weight α. At α = 0 the species ignore each other and all coexist; as α climbs past the ratio of the weakest capacity to the strongest, the weak start being excluded. That is the whole x-axis of the phase diagram.

Both coupling modes share every loss term. Pollinators graze producers through a Holling type II response and birds eat pollinators through another; those terms are byte-identical between modes. The modes differ in one line only — what sets animal numbers. Funded: n += n·(e·intake − upkeep), energy accounting, an individual that cannot pay dies. Ceiling: n += r·(K − n) with K = e·(biomass below), a rate, nothing starves, the ceiling moves. The rng is consumed in the same order and the same quantity either way, so the two runs on a given seed see the same noise realisation. The comparison is genuinely paired.

Extinction is handled differently by level, on purpose. A producer species that drops below its threshold is gone for good — no seed bank in this bench, which is exactly the assumption the phase-spread result indicts. Animal levels get a tiny propagule floor instead, so they can recolonise; what is measured there is not extinction but presence: the share of ticks the level is above threshold, and the number of times it crossed from present to absent. Thresholds are 2% of each level's nominal standing crop, so no level is being held to a harsher bar than another.

On the 10% number. The default is set at Pauly & Christensen's measured mean of 10.13% (Nature, 1995; 140 trophic-transfer estimates from 48 ecosystem models, s.d. 5.8) — a genuinely useful central value, and the reason two levels of stacking lands the top of a small island at about one individual. But the folk version of it, "10% per level is why food chains are short", is not well supported: Post (2002) reviews the evidence and finds ecosystem size predicts chain length better than either energy supply or productivity. Which is a design note as much as an ecology note — if chain length tracks size, then a bigger island earning another trophic level is a defensible mechanic, and a small island simply should not have one.

What this does and does not tell us about Wonder

Does: that at a defensible transfer efficiency, a two-step chain over a small energy base lands the top level at roughly one individual, and that in that regime an energy-funded top level is not a population but a coin flip. That result is robust — it survives zero noise, the whole upkeep range, and every forcing period tested. It is a structural consequence of stacking multiplicative growth on a thin base, so it will show up in any implementation that funds individuals directly.

Does not: anything spatial. No tiles, no dispersal, no refugia, no travelling waves — and refugia are the classic way real predator–prey systems survive exactly the troughs that kill this one. No evolution: uptake efficiencies are fixed at seed time, so nothing adapts to the forcing, which is precisely the mechanism that would let a temporal niche pay. No stage structure, so no storage effect. And no individuals: these are continuous densities, and "0.4 birds" is a fiction that a discrete-agent implementation would resolve differently, probably less kindly.

What a player would actually see. Under the ceiling model, an island that breathes: bloom-day comes, the flowers thicken, a week later there are visibly more pollinators, and a week after that the birds are easier to find. Nothing dies on screen. The chain reads as a rate — abundance sliding up and down with the season — which is the only version compatible with Wonder's peaceful pillar, and, usefully, the version that is also more legible: a slow swell is readable, a stochastic death is not. Under the funded model, the top predator blinks out roughly twenty times per thousand ticks. A player who has spent an hour learning to find one bird would watch the species vanish and reappear with no cause they could perceive or act on, and they would not file that as ecology. They would file it as the game being broken.

The one thing the ceiling model gives up is the drama of a real crash, and it should be said plainly: nothing in this design can produce a collapse the player caused and could have prevented. If Wonder ever wants that, it has to come from somewhere other than the trophic arithmetic — and it should be deliberate, rare, and legible, not an emergent side effect of dividing by ten twice.
