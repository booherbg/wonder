The reagent economy

A world rolls its own chemistry. A plant inherits a small hand of conversions and runs it once, at maturity. What comes out is energy, a pigment, and a body — all three from the same computation. Nothing here is decorative: every colour on this page below the rule was earned by a chain that actually fired.

The system

A world generates a set of reagents and a deck of conversion recipes. A plant is a hand of three to five of those recipes, drawn at birth. Running that hand against the reagents present in its soil produces a pile — the multiset of reagents it is left holding. Energy, colour and body shape are all read off that one pile and the sequence of conversions that filled it. The run happens once, at maturity, and is cached; nothing here is per-tick.

Parts

reagent
A named substance. Carries a tier (1–4) and a fixed hue. About ten per world, named from a shared pool so two islands never share a chemistry.
tier
Rank of a reagent: 1 base, 2 refined, 3 compound, 4 essence. Tier sets three numbers — energy per unit [1, 3, 9, 27], pigment weight [1, 7, 40, 180], and the chroma ceiling. Pigment weight climbs far faster than energy on purpose: the scarce thing should be what you see.
catalyst
One or two mid-tier reagents flagged at worldgen. A catalyse recipe requires one to be present and never consumes it, so a catalyst is worth having nearby rather than worth spending.
card
One conversion recipe. Four kinds. gather: produce 3–6 units of one base from nothing. upgrade: spend any cost units held at tier t for one unit of a specific tier t+1 reagent — generic in its input, which is what makes a randomly drawn hand chainable. trade: spend 2–3 of A and 1–2 of B for one of something higher. catalyse: spend 1–2 of one reagent for 1–2 of the next tier, but only while its catalyst is in the pile.
deck
Every recipe this world offers: one gather per base reagent, one generic upgrade per reagent above tier 1, five rolled trades, and two rolled catalyse cards per catalyst.
soil
What the ground offers before anything grows: two base reagents at 2–4 units each, and a 50% chance of a single unit of a catalyst. The chain starts from this.
hand
The genome. Three to five cards drawn from the deck, always including exactly one gather, held in a fixed order. Plus a step budget of 8–36 conversions.
pile
The reagents currently held, as counts per reagent. Starts as a copy of the soil; every conversion spends from it and adds to it. The pile at the moment the chain stops is what the colour and the energy are computed from.

Procedure

  1. Roll the world. From the seed: reagent names, tiers, and hues drawn from a single anchor ± half the hue spread — one narrow spread is what makes an island tonal rather than polychrome. Flag the catalysts, build the deck, roll the soil.
  2. Roll the plant. Draw the hand and the step budget.
  3. Run the chain. Copy the soil into the pile, then walk the hand in genome order, firing every card whose inputs are held. Repeat until a full pass fires nothing or the budget is spent. A gather idles while the pile already holds its output plus the caravan headroom, so the chain must spend before it can draw again — that constraint is what stops every plant hoarding base.
  4. Score it. energy = Σ units × tier energy; cost = 4 + 1.2 × steps; yield ratio is energy over cost.
  5. Derive the pigment. Hue is the circular mean of the held reagents' hues, each weighted by units × tier pigment weight. Chroma comes from the pigment-weighted mean tier (not the highest tier held), scaled by how well the held hues agree. Lightness is a curve on the yield ratio.
  6. Grow the body. Replay the same run; each conversion emits one growth instruction as it fires. Gather extends the tips, upgrade puts out a side branch while the leader keeps growing, trade adds a whorl of leaves, catalyse swells a node. A product of tier 2 or better, late in the chain, terminates in a bloom. The roots are the first half of the same chain run downward.

Try this first

  1. Press Roll a new world two or three times. Each press regenerates the whole chemistry — different reagents, different hues, different recipes — so the shelf of plants at the bottom changes key entirely.
  2. Click any plant on that shelf. Its hand, its run and its body appear above. Read the run log top to bottom: that is the plant's whole life, about ten lines long.
  3. Drag pigment steepness down to 0.30. Every plant turns to mud, because the heap of leftover base now outvotes the rare stuff. Drag it to 2.00 and a single unit of essence paints an entire plant. That one dial is the argument that colour should have to be earned.

A plant is a list of card indices into the world's deck, plus one integer. That is all. It is not a list of traits, and nothing in it mentions colour, height or shape.

      units × tier pigment weight. Hue is the angle of the sum; coherence is

Why it is built this way

    

      Chroma reads the pigment-weighted mean tier, not the highest tier held.
      One stray unit of essence sitting in a heap of base scores a mean tier near 1 and comes
      out sage or ochre; a plant that is mostly essence scores near 4 and comes out a jewel
      tone. The alternative — reading the ceiling — would paint every plant that ever touched
      tier 4 the same, and the shelf would lose its spread. Hue is weighted by
      units × tier pigment weight, with those weights at 1/7/40/180 against
      energy's 1/3/9/27, so the scarce reagent colours the plant rather than the leftover base
      it is sitting on. Lightness is yield over cost, so a plant in soil its hand cannot use
      pales.
    

    

      There is no separate morphology genome. Each conversion emits one
      growth instruction as it fires, so a plant that runs a nine-step chain draws nine
      instructions and a plant that runs three draws three. Roots are the first half of the
      same program run downward. Structure and metabolism cannot disagree, because there is
      only one of them.
    

    

      Growth costs nothing extra to animate. Press Watch it grow
      and the specimen redraws one conversion at a time — the same chain, paced over 1.5
      seconds rather than run in a single frame. Measured on the default seed the shoot grows
      5× in ink over that window, monotonically. Wonder's plants currently appear fully
      formed, which discards the only visible record of which step put out the leaves and
      which one opened the bloom. Reduced-motion is honoured; the plate draws finished.
    

    

      A catalyse card checks the pile for its catalyst and does not spend it.
      That single rule is what makes neighbours worth having: whatever first leaks a catalyst
      onto a tile opens a branch of chemistry for everything that grows there afterwards.
      Cards that need one are marked in the run log.
    

    

      Worth trying: roll worlds until you find one with a narrow hue spread — those come out
      tonal. Wide-spread worlds go polychrome. Then look for a plant that reached tier four;
      it should be the only thing on the shelf you cannot stop looking at. If it is not, the
      chroma ladder needs work, which is what this bench is for.
