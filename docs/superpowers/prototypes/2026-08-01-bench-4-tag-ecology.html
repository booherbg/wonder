Tag ecology: all of ECHO, not one slice of it

Wonder already ships Holland's tag matching — an insect's sensor map adapts toward a flower's appearance map, and a reward economy pays out on the match. It works, and we use it for exactly one relationship. This bench runs the whole of ECHO instead: four resources, four tag strings per agent, and a chromosome written in the resource alphabet so that reproduction costs precisely the letters you are made of. The question is whether trophic layers, mimicry and arms races fall out of tag matching on their own — and whether any of it survives being made peaceful.

This world's four resources

Each resource presents an appearance tag. An agent's sensor tag is matched against all four; uptake scales as match²⋅₅, so gathering is a specialist's game and no single agent can farm all four letters well. Since a chromosome costs the letters it is written in, almost every lineage needs letters it cannot gather — that is the pressure that invents trade and predation.

Numbers below are means over seven seeds × 2500 ticks at the default settings (inflow 24, tag length 6, mutation 0.03, threshold 1.0×, 400 interactions/tick, combat bias 0.45, upkeep 0.02, cap 400). Statistics are averaged over the second half of each run. They were measured through the same code path the page runs, not a separate model.

Trophic layers appear, and they need combat to do it. With combat bias at exactly zero the ecosystem is perfectly flat in every run: mean trophic level 1.000, layer spread 0.000, and 0 of 7 seeds ever meet the layering criterion. Turn combat on and species separate by how they make a living — the most autotrophic sizeable species takes 29% of its intake off other agents while the most predatory takes 69%, a spread of 0.414. Mean trophic level settles at 2.04, peak 3.29. 7 of 7 seeds reach sustained layering, at t ≈ 387 on average — earliest t = 171, latest t = 851 — and hold it for the rest of the run. It is a steady state, not a transient.

The arms race is real and it is the cleanest signal on the page. The population-weighted score an attacker's appearance gets against a defender's defence tag starts at 0.354 and falls to 0.276 — defence outrunning offence by 22%. The control is what makes it a claim: with combat bias at zero the same number drifts down only 0.012 over the same 2500 ticks, so the combat-driven fall of 0.078 is six times what drift alone produces. Its late-run standard deviation is 0.0175 against the control's 0.0068 — the score does not settle, it keeps being knocked back up as offence re-finds the new defences. That is Red Queen behaviour, from tag matching alone, with nothing in the model that mentions predators.

The arms race has a signature you can read off the ledger with your eyes. Defence tags go monotone. Measured as the share of a tag taken by its commonest letter, defence sits at 0.488 with combat off — chance, for a random tag over four letters — and climbs to 0.852 with combat on, while appearance tags in the same runs stay at 0.467. Tag lengths barely move (6.07 vs 6.31), so this is not padding, it is composition: defences collapse onto ccccc, bbbbbbbb, ddddddd. It works because half of the match score is the longest unbroken run, and a monotone defence denies a run to every attacker whose appearance is mixed. The counter exists — a monotone attacker scores a perfect 1.0 against the matching monotone defence — but it is useless against everything else, so the two keep displacing each other. That is the oscillation. Part of this is a real result and part is my scoring choice showing through, and the page should say so: a different match rule would produce a different exploit. What would not change is that something gets exploited, quickly, and only when losing carries a cost. In rates-only mode defence uniformity reaches just 0.534, barely off the no-combat control.

Mimicry did not happen. This is the claim the bench most expected to confirm and it does not. Raw appearance-tag similarity between distant species does hit 0.8–1.0, but that number is worthless: with 30–65 species there are hundreds of pairs, and the maximum of anything over hundreds of pairs is high by luck. Scored against the internal control — the same statistic computed on the two private tags, which nobody can imitate because nobody can see them — the excess is −0.021. Negative. Public tags converge no more than private ones. Transient candidate pairs do appear (peak edge 0.219) but they last a handful of samples and clear the threshold in only 11% of them. Sweeping tag length 3–12, mutation 0.005–0.08 and combat bias 0.15–1.0 never lifted the mean edge above 0.054. Under these rules, looking like someone else does not pay enough.

The decisive result: it is not death that does the work, it is a full world. Lethal mode and quiet mode produced bit-identical results across all seven seeds — every statistic on this page agrees to the last digit — because at any survivable upkeep, starved = 0. Nothing ever actually starves. All the selection was coming from displacement at carrying capacity. Push upkeep high enough for starvation to fire (0.6, thirty times the default) and it does not select, it simply exterminates: population 0 within 600 ticks, against 13 survivors in the two non-lethal modes at the same setting. There is no regime where dying of hunger does anything useful.

Rates-only keeps a shadow of it. Remove displacement too — nothing removed for being poor, poverty only slowing reproduction — and the structure degrades but does not vanish. Layer spread falls 0.414 → 0.361, and the more telling number is the floor: the most autotrophic species goes from taking 29% of its intake off others to taking 44%, so the producer tier stops being distinct. All seven seeds still reach the layering criterion, but half again as slowly (t ≈ 594 against 387, latest 1461). The arms race is the real casualty, and the number is blunt: attack score moves 0.356 → 0.349, a fall of 0.0073 — smaller than the no-combat control's 0.0123. Its oscillation, 0.0034, is half the control's. By both measures there is not a weak arms race in rates-only mode; there is none at all, and the tags are doing what they would do if combat had been switched off. Meanwhile species count nearly doubles, 35 to 65, and Shannon H rises 2.94 to 3.86. That looks like more biodiversity and is the opposite: with selection weakened, lineages drift apart into interchangeable near-duplicates. Turnover halves too — 3,653 births against 7,333 — so the world evolves at half speed per tick.

Most interesting regime. Combat bias 0.25–0.5 with 400 interactions/tick. Below that the world is all trade and perfectly flat; above it every species robs every other and the layering collapses back into mush at a high but meaningless trophic level (at combat 1.0 with 900 interactions/tick, mean TL reads 5.03 while layer spread falls to 0.28 — deep-looking and structureless). Long tags are a cliff: at tag length 12 with combat 0.5 the world goes extinct, because a 48-letter chromosome costs 48 letters to copy and predation on top of that is unpayable.
