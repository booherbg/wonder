Is 30×50 big enough?

The host view replaces the plant stat card with a picture: a plant drawn as a botanical plate, its flowers and leaves visible, its pollinators actually perched on it, and — the part that is otherwise invisible — its roots drawn over the mineral field they are eating. Blaine sized it at "30×50 or so" and asked whether that is enough. This bench renders the same generated specimen across a ladder of plate resolutions at true pixel scale so the question can be answered by pointing rather than by arguing. The chemistry, the pigment and the grow-the-body-from-the-chain program are the ones from the reagent-economy bench; only the display surface is new.

The system

Not a simulation — a rendering test. It takes the reagent economy's generated specimen, its earned pigment and its grow-the-body-from-the-chain program unchanged, and draws them into a fixed pixel budget. Every plate on this page is rasterised at its true size and then upscaled with nearest-neighbour, so what you are judging is the real pixel count and never an approximation of it. The question is where in the resolution ladder the picture stops improving.

Parts

plate
The whole drawing surface: W wide, Hs of shoot above the ground line, and a root pane below it sized as a percentage of the shoot. The proposal under test is 30 × 50 for the shoot; the doc's full-plate proposal is 30 × 74.
ground line
The horizontal divide. Above it, stem, leaves and flowers. Below it, the roots drawn over the mineral field they are drawing from — the part of a plant that is otherwise entirely invisible in the game.
specimen
A plant from bench 0: a rolled world chemistry, a rolled hand, and the chain those produce. The specimen dial picks a different plant from the same island chemistry rather than a new world.
growth instruction
What each conversion in the chain emits when it fires — extend, branch, whorl of leaves, swollen node, bloom. The plate is drawn by replaying those, so its structure is not decorative.
insect
A perched pollinator drawn at the game's own ratio: 5 × W / 16 pixels long. At W = 30 that is 9 px, which is the number that made the paper estimate of "2–3 px" wrong by 3×.
upscale
Nearest-neighbour display magnification only. It changes nothing about the pixel budget — it exists so you can see individual pixels without a magnifier.
ink coverage
The fraction of the plate that is not background. It is the measurement that settles the ladder, because it falls as the plate grows: a bigger plate is mostly bigger margins.
distinctness
Whether two perched insects remain separable at a given size. This is what caps a plate at two pollinators regardless of resolution, and it is crowding rather than legibility.

Procedure

  1. Roll the world chemistry and the plant, and run its chain — the bench-0 pipeline, unmodified.
  2. Derive the palette: pigment from the chain, leaf and highlight variants from it, and a soil palette pulled toward the terrain.
  3. Allocate the plate, draw the mineral field, then the roots over it, then the shoot above the line, replaying the chain's growth instructions.
  4. Perch the requested insects on real attachment points, at true scale.
  5. Rasterise once per rung of the resolution ladder and upscale each for display.
  6. Measure ink coverage, feature counts and insect distinctness on every rung.

The resolution ladder — the same specimen, five plate budgets

One plant, one chain, one soil field, rendered at five plate resolutions and upscaled to the same display height so they are honestly comparable. Rows are 1, 3 and 8 perched insects, each drawn at the size the game's own sprite ratio implies for that plate (5 × W / 16, because a world plant sprite is 16×28 px and the inked insect inside its 7×7 canvas is about 5×4). Non-integer upscales mean some pixel rows land a hair taller than others; the alternative was to make the plates different sizes, which would have answered nothing.

The same pollinator, drawn at body lengths 2 to 13 plate px, magnified 6× on a common tile so the sizes compare. The verdict line asks one question: can you still see a body axis, a head and a wing? Under it, the same ladder at 1:1 — that is the honest size on the plate.

Measurements

Measured off the pixel buffers themselves, not estimated. Ink is the share of the shoot pane carrying plant pixels. Flowers fit and insects fit are swept: the largest count at which every one of them still lands in its own 4-connected component, so two blooms that merge into one smear count as one. Separable @8 is the same test held at eight insects, and occluded is how much of the plant those eight cover up. Bands is how many distinct mineral runs a vertical cut through the middle of the root pane crosses — below three, the field is not a pattern any more, it is a wash.

How to read this

The plate is one image with two panes. Above the ground line is the shoot — stem, leaves, blooms, berries — and below it the roots, drawn over the mineral field. The field is a world-space function sampled per plate pixel, so every plate in the ladder is looking at the same patch of ground at a different pixel density. That is the only way the comparison means anything.

Nothing here has a morphology genome. The body is the metabolic chain run as a growth program, exactly as the reagent-economy bench does it: a gather extends a segment, an upgrade forks a branch at an angle taken from the reagent's hue, a trade puts out a whorl of leaves, a catalyse swells a node, and a late high-tier product terminates in a bloom. The roots are the same program run downward — with one addition that is the point of this bench: root tips are tropic. At each step a tip scores three candidate directions by how much of its own affinity mineral lies ahead, and takes the best. Where a root pixel lands on a mineral it can eat, that pixel is painted in that mineral's colour rather than in root colour. The bright specks in the lower pane are literally the plant feeding.

The pigment is the circular mean of what it ended up holding, weighted steeply by tier, so the scarce thing colours the plant. Foliage carries the same hue pulled part-way toward a chlorophyll anchor — chlorophyll is a constant in this world; the metabolism is what gets added to it. A chain that reaches tier 4 does not buy a more saturated pigment, it buys iridescence, and the blooms shimmer between two hues.

The insects wear their own metabolism. Each perched pollinator keeps a random digestible subset of the host's pile and runs the same pigment function over it, so two insects on one flower can come out different colours. That is the doc's claim about insects transforming colour rather than copying it, made visible: the strip in row one is the same plant seen through several different guts. Measured over 800 guts, it moves the hue a median of 5.4° — so the honest version of that claim is that most insects end up wearing their host, and only the tail genuinely diverges. That is why the sprite here is pushed dark with a light wing: on a plate, a pollinator cannot be made visible by hue when hue is the thing it inherited.

What this does not tell us. The plate is a still. It says nothing about whether insects arriving and leaving reads as motion at these sizes, and motion is usually what rescues a small sprite. It also draws perched insects from a static roll rather than from live swarm state — deliberately, per the doc's "make the host view a view, not a place" — so the crowding numbers here are a ceiling test, not a prediction of typical occupancy. And the plant is one specimen; a plate that reads well for a tall branching herb may read badly for a moss or a tree, which are the two ends this suite has not sampled.

What the player would actually see. They click a plant and get this plate. In two seconds they can see what colour it is, how many flowers it is offering, how many pollinators are currently on it and what colour they are — and, in the lower pane, whether this plant's roots are actually finding what they eat or are threaded blindly through ground that has nothing for them. A plant in the wrong soil has a pale shoot and a lower pane with almost no bright specks in it. That is a diagnosis with no numbers in it at all.

Findings

74 islands × 5 plate resolutions, root pane at 48% of the shoot, insects drawn at the game's own ratio (5 × W / 16). Flowers and insects are the largest count at which every one of them still owns a separate component after one pixel of dilation — the point at which you can still count them by eye. Yes — 30×50 is enough for the shoot, and the thing it runs out of is not what anyone expected. At 30×50 the whole body plan reads: the leader, the branch order, the leaf whorls, a median of seven individually countable blooms, the berries, the swollen catalyst nodes. Going to 64×104 costs 4.4× the pixels and buys two more flowers. It does not buy a better plant — it buys more paper, which is what the ink numbers say out loud: the shoot's coverage falls from 12.8% to 9.3% as the plate grows, because the plant is a line drawing and lines do not thicken when the canvas does. The one real gain from a bigger plate is insect room, and it is small (see below).

The doc's 2–3 px insect estimate was wrong by about 3×, and that resolves the legibility question in 30×50's favour. A world plant sprite is 16×28 px (plantSprites.ts:6) and the inked insect inside its 7×7 canvas is about 5×4 (insectSprites.ts:15). Holding that ratio, a perched insect on a 30-wide plate is 9 px, not 2–3. On the ladder above, 6 px is the floor — that is where the head lifts clear of the wing — and 8 px is where the body plan is unambiguous (two wing rows, belly, three legs). True scale crosses 6 px at plate width 20 and 8 px at plate width 26. So single-insect legibility is satisfied comfortably below Blaine's proposal and is not the constraint on plate size. What the plate cannot do is teach the seventeen insect forms apart; that is the examine card's job and it already upscales 12×.

Insects are the budget, and the budget barely moves with resolution. At 30×50 the median plate holds two pollinators before they merge — 45 of 74 islands fit exactly 2, 22 fit 3, 6 fit only 1. Ask for eight and the median number you can still tell apart is one, with a quarter of the plant hidden under them. And the ceiling hardly rises: 64×104 has 4.4× the pixels and still fits only three, because true-scale insects grow with the plate. This is the sharpest result on the page. A host plate cannot show a swarm. If the design wants a busy flower it has to either break true scale — draw insects smaller than the world ratio, which will look wrong the moment a player sees the same species in the world — or stop drawing individuals past three and show the rest as a count.

The root pane works, and it needed two decisions to work. The mineral field resolves at every size tested: a median of 6 distinct mineral runs down a centre cut at both 24×40 and 30×50, with only 3 of 74 islands falling below four. But it only resolves because of two things that were not obvious going in. First, the six minerals cannot be separated by hue. Wonder rolls hue spreads as narrow as 70°, and six hues inside 70° are one colour at 30 px wide — the ground has to carry a per-world value ladder, one lightness step per mineral, and the hue rides on top. Second, the matched root pixel has to be the brightest thing in the pane and has to wear the mineral's own colour; a root drawn in one colour over soil drawn in another disappears the moment the world rolls those two colours close together. With both in place the match is genuinely diagnostic: across islands the matched share of root length runs from 1% to 96% (mean 44%), so a plant in the wrong ground looks obviously different from one in the right ground, with no numbers on screen.

The root pane's real problem is emptiness, not resolution. At 30×50 + 24 the roots lay down a median 174 px over a 720 px pane — they touch 24% of the ground they are drawn over, and that falls to 13% at 64×104. Most of the lower pane is a picture of soil the plant never reaches. That argues for a shallower root pane than the 48% used here, or for roots that spread wider rather than deeper.

An insect that wears its host's metabolism is camouflaged on its host. Running the doc's §13 rule literally — the insect metabolises a digestible subset of the nectar and takes the pigment of what it kept — the median gut lands 5.4° of hue from the host's own pigment, with 71% inside 15° and 82% inside 30° (n = 800 guts over 200 islands). Only the tail diverges: the 90th percentile is 59°. Median lightness moves 0.07. So "insects transform colour rather than copy it" is true about one insect in five and false about the rest, and on the plate that means hue cannot be what makes a perched insect visible — this bench had to push the body dark and the wing light to make them read at all. That is not a drawing problem, it is the mechanic: it means crypsis is the default outcome of the colour system, which is excellent news for the bird-hunts-what-stands-out idea and bad news for any UI that expects the player to spot pollinators by colour.

Recommendation. Ship the plate at 30×74 — 30 wide, 50 for the shoot, a ground line, 23 for the roots — exactly as the doc proposed. The aspect ratio does not want to change: the roots do not need more than about 45% of the shoot's height, and every pixel past that is soil nothing touches. Budget the plate for two to three perched insects, not eight, and treat that as a design constraint on the pollination model rather than a rendering detail. If a later build wants pollinators to be individually identifiable on the host, that is a 40×64 plate (insect 13 px) and it costs 71% more pixels for one extra legible insect — worth it only if landing decisions really do become per-flower.

What would change this. One specimen archetype was measured — a branching herb. A moss (few steps, no branching) and a tree (many steps, five tips) sit at the ends this bench did not sample, and the tree is the one that worries me: five tips at 30 px wide is 6 px of separation between branches. And the plate is a still. Motion is usually what rescues a small sprite, and a pollinator that arrives, lands and leaves may well read at 5 px where a frozen one does not.
