Motion signature: the phenotype you can still see at five pixels

Every trait we have designed for Wonder so far is a still picture — colour, pattern, body shape. But the game is watched in motion, and at normal zoom an insect is about five pixels across, which is roughly the size at which colour stops being reliable and shape stops existing. What survives that zoom is how the thing moves. This bench gives a lineage a seven-gene flight genome, asks whether a person can tell those genomes apart at gameplay scale, measures the same question objectively with a classifier, and then asks the design question: if a predator reads movement as well as appearance, is a swarm forced to choose between moving like food and moving like the wind?

The system

A flight model with a seven-gene genome, and three tests run against it. Insects fly in a meadow with a wind field, drifting clutter and a predator; the genome decides how they move and nothing else. Test one asks whether you can tell lineages apart at gameplay scale. Test two asks the same question objectively, with a nearest-neighbour classifier on measured trajectory features. Test three asks whether moving in a way that feeds well and moving in a way that avoids the predator are the same thing.

Parts

genome
Seven continuous genes. speed cruise px/s · dart the impulse a dart adds instantly · rate darts per second, Poisson so the spacing is irregular · hover seconds parked after a dart, which is what makes a track dotted · turn how fast heading snaps to target, high being angular and low being long smooth arcs · curve constant rad/s added to heading, which loops the track · wind coupling from 0 (flies its own line) to 1 (mostly carried).
archetype
Eight named genomes — Skipper, Hoverfly, Drifter, Spiral, Sawtooth, Bobber, Ranger, Tumbler — used as the fixed reference set so the identification test has a known chance level of 1 in 8.
wind field
A smooth time-varying vector field over the meadow. It carries the clutter exactly, which makes it the reference the predator compares everything against.
clutter
Drifting leaves that move exactly with the field. They dilute the predator's attention, so moving like the wind is a real strategy rather than a metaphor.
hazard
Detection pressure on one insect: how far its motion departs from the local wind, scaled by predator sensitivity and reduced by the masking the clutter provides.
forage score
Flowers visited per unit time under the same genome. A fast, straight flier feeds well and is trivially visible; a drifter is invisible and starves.
trajectory features
What the classifier actually sees: speed statistics, turn-rate statistics, the stop fraction, path sinuosity, and the peak of the movement's frequency spectrum. Z-scored, then classified by leave-one-out k-nearest-neighbour, so the accuracy number is out-of-sample.
sprite scale
Screen pixels across. Five is Wonder's normal zoom, and it is the size at which the colour channel is compared against the motion channel.

Procedure

  1. Fly every lineage in the shared meadow: each insect steers toward the nearest flower, darts on its Poisson schedule, hovers, and is pushed by the wind in proportion to its coupling gene.
  2. The human test. Show an unlabelled swarm at the current sprite size and record whether you name it correctly, against a 1-in-8 chance level.
  3. The classifier. Sample trajectories from random genomes, extract features, z-score them, and measure leave-one-out accuracy. Also compute the colour channel's accuracy at the same sprite size, so the two can be compared on one axis.
  4. The trade-off. Fly many random genomes, score each on feeding and on hazard, and extract the Pareto front. Switch the predator off and watch the front collapse to a single optimum — that collapse is the evidence that the trade-off is what generates variety.
  5. The lineage strip. Mutate a genome repeatedly at the current step size and draw the resulting tracks, so heritability of motion is visible directly.

Try this first

  1. Set sprite scale to 5 — Wonder's normal zoom — and take the identification test. Then set it to 12 and take it again. The difference is the whole point.
  2. Compare the two curves in the motion-versus-colour chart and find where they cross. Below that size, how a thing moves is the more reliable signal.
  3. Turn predator sensitivity to 0 and watch the trade-off scatter collapse to a single optimum. Without something hunting, there is only one right way to fly.

Every insect here is drawn at the sprite size the slider says — five screen pixels by default. Nothing else about them differs except their flight genome and their hue. Watch it for thirty seconds without reading the labels and see whether the swarms separate for you.

2 · The trace — a signature as a picture—

The same genomes, flown on an open plane with the same wind, drawn as persistent tracks. Fit each track normalises every cell to its own bounding box so you compare shape; the scale bar in each cell tells you how much ground that shape actually covers. One common scale shows all of them at the same magnification, which is the honest picture of how differently these things get around.

7 · Lineage strip — does a mutation move the signature?—

Leftmost cell is the focused lineage. The six to its right are offspring with every gene perturbed by a Gaussian of the mutation size in the rail. The number under each is how far that offspring moved in the same normalised feature space the classifier uses, expressed as a fraction of the mean distance between two different lineages. Under about 0.3 the child is still recognisably its parent; over 1.0 the mutation has invented a new signature.
